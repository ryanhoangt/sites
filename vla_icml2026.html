Most snapshots of a research field are taken from the submission pile. That is the natural place to look: submissions are numerous, they arrive early, and on OpenReview they are public. Moritz Reuss’s analysis of 164 Vision-Language-Action submissions to ICLR 2026 is the best recent example, and this piece is a deliberate companion to it.

But a submission pile answers a different question than an accept list does. Submissions tell you what the community tried. Accepts tell you what a few thousand hours of adversarial peer review decided was worth keeping. The gap between those two things is where a field’s actual selection pressure becomes visible — and that pressure shapes what gets built next far more than the submission distribution does, because it determines what gets funded, cited, and continued.

This is an analysis of 69 VLA papers accepted to ICML 2026. Read on its own, it is a map of a field that has quietly reorganized itself around problems that were barely visible eighteen months ago. Read against the ICLR submission pile, it is something sharper: evidence that the loudest trends in VLA research and the most durable ones are not the same set.

Three findings drive everything below.

Latent action representations, not action tokenizers, are the field’s center of gravity. Fifteen of 69 papers work on them. The question of how to construct an intermediate representation between pixels and motor commands — one that survives transfer across embodiments, datasets, and modalities — has absorbed more of this corpus than any other single problem.

The benchmark crisis got worse, in an unexpected direction. The standard complaint about VLA evaluation is saturation: LIBERO is solved, CALVIN is nearly solved, comparisons are meaningless near the ceiling. That complaint is correct and this corpus confirms it. But the corpus’s response to saturation was not consolidation around better shared benchmarks. It was proliferation: ten of 69 papers introduce a new benchmark, dataset, or diagnostic suite of their own. The field is replacing an unusable common yardstick with a dozen incompatible private ones.

Safety, interpretability, and data quality are close to absent. One paper on adversarial robustness, and it is an attack. One on interpretability. Four with a serious data-centric contribution. Whatever review is selecting for, it is not these.

01Method, and what this analysis cannot do

The corpus is 69 papers scraped from the ICML 2026 accept list, filtered for Vision-Language-Action keywords, with title, authors, area/subarea, presentation format, OpenReview and virtual-site links, arXiv identifiers where available, and abstracts.

I read all 69 abstracts and coded each one by hand along four axes: a primary research cluster, any secondary clusters, a single intervention point on the VLA stack, and evaluation attributes (benchmarks named, strength of real-robot evidence, whether a new evaluation artifact is introduced). Counts throughout are from that hand-coding, not keyword matching; keyword matching on abstracts produces systematically wrong numbers here, because abstracts use “efficient,” “reasoning,” and “generalization” as ambient vocabulary rather than as claims.

Five limitations bound every claim that follows, and they are worth stating plainly rather than burying.

Abstracts only. No PDFs were read. This means no verified numbers, no ablation inspection, no assessment of whether a method’s stated mechanism matches its implementation, and no ability to catch the gap between an abstract’s claim and a paper’s evidence. Where I report a performance figure, it is the authors’ self-reported figure and should be read as a claim, not a result. Abstracts systematically overstate: they report best-case numbers, name the friendliest baselines, and omit failure modes. Every quantitative statement below inherits that bias.

Keyword-scoped corpus. The corpus contains papers that use VLA vocabulary. Work that contributes to the same problems without that vocabulary — diffusion policy papers, imitation learning theory, manipulation-specific representation learning, most of the 3D policy literature — is invisible here. This matters most for the gap analysis: an absence in this corpus is an absence of VLA-branded work on a topic, not an absence of work.

Single venue, single cycle. ICML has a distinct reviewer population and distinct taste. It has historically favored method papers with a clean formal story over systems papers with large empirical footprints. Some of what looks like a field-wide trend below may be an ICML trend. The cross-venue section addresses this directly but cannot fully resolve it.

Accept lists are censored data. We see what got in. We do not see the rejected papers, so we cannot distinguish “the field stopped working on X” from “the field kept working on X and reviewers stopped accepting it.” These are very different diagnoses with opposite implications for a researcher choosing a direction. Where the distinction matters, I flag it.

No affiliation data. The scrape’s institution field is empty for all 69 papers. There is no lab-level, country-level, or industry-versus-academia analysis here. Given how central the frontier-lab-versus-academic-lab gap is to the current VLA conversation, this is the most painful missing axis.

One more: all 69 papers are listed as posters, with 8 flagged as spotlights. There is no oral tier in this data, so presentation format carries almost no signal beyond the spotlight flag.

02Corpus anatomy

Sixty-nine papers, eight spotlights (11.6%). The subject-area distribution is lopsided in a way that is itself informative: 64 of 69 sit under Applications, and 59 of those under Robotics. VLA work is being filed as an application of machine learning rather than as machine learning.

The five papers filed elsewhere are the interesting ones, because they mark where VLA research is starting to touch other communities. There is one under Social Aspects/Security — an adversarial attack on chain-of-thought reasoning in VLAs. One under Evaluation. One under Deep Learning/Foundation Models. Two under Computer Vision. That is the entire footprint of VLA research outside the robotics subarea. The field talks constantly about being a foundation-model discipline; institutionally, it is still filed as robotics.

Authorship is strikingly flat. 534 unique authors across 69 papers. The most prolific author appears on five; the next on four; then a short tail of threes and twos, and then hundreds of single appearances. There is no dominant lab visible in this slice — no group with the kind of concentration that would let you say a particular institution owns a research direction. For a field often described as compute-gated and concentrated, the publication surface at ICML looks unusually distributed. That is consistent with a low-barrier entry point: most of these papers modify a released open-weight backbone rather than pretraining one.

Openness is weak, at least by the measure available. Nine of 69 abstracts contain a GitHub link; 22 contain any URL at all. This is a floor rather than an estimate — many papers release code without advertising it in the abstract — but the ratio is low enough to be worth noting against a field that depends on shared backbones. Papers that build on open releases are not reliably producing open releases in turn.

03A structural taxonomy: five places to intervene

Trend lists are the standard way to organize a survey, and they have a specific failure: they organize by what people did, which tells a reader what is crowded but not where the room is. Nine parallel trend buckets with paper counts attached will tell you that reasoning is popular. It will not tell you that nobody is working on the interaction between reasoning and memory.

A more useful frame for this corpus: every VLA paper modifies the same pipeline, and it modifies it at one of five points.

Representation (13 papers) — what the model encodes. Interventions on the input side and on the internal state: 3D point clouds instead of or alongside 2D images (Any3D-VLA), persistent spatial memory built from multi-view scanning (SOMA), hierarchical memory split across frequency bands (HiMe), spiking neural representations for energy efficiency (SpikeVLA), unified discrete codes that jointly encode visual dynamics and robot motion (XR-1), information-bottleneck filtering of visual noise (StableVLA).

Action interface (7 papers) — how continuous control gets expressed. The smallest group and the most theoretically loaded. This is where the field argues about the fundamental mismatch: robots take continuous, high-rate commands; pretrained language backbones predict discrete tokens. Papers here either build a better bridge (LAST’s Lie-algebraic tokenizer, RoboOmni’s multi-token action prediction, Discrete Diffusion VLA’s parallel token decoding) or reject the bridge entirely (NIAF replaces discrete waypoints with continuous action fields; GAM reformulates the action space as a geometry-respecting manifold).

Objective (23 papers) — what gets supervised. The largest group by a clear margin. Latent action supervision, future-frame prediction, world-model co-training, contrastive alignment to proprioceptive state, RL fine-tuning, mutual-information objectives that penalize language-ignoring shortcuts. The dominance of this layer is the corpus’s single most important structural fact and I return to it below.

Inference (17 papers) — what happens at run time. Token pruning, channel pruning, KV-cache-compatible streaming, speculative decoding, early exit from latent reasoning, uncertainty-triggered deliberation, retrieval of in-context demonstrations, test-time search over trajectory distributions, policy ensembling. Notably, a large fraction of this group is explicitly training-free, which is a meaningful signal about what a compute-constrained field converges on.

Evaluation (9 papers) — what progress is measured against. New benchmarks, diagnostic suites, fault taxonomies, data generators, interpretability tooling, and one security paper that is best understood as an evaluation contribution (it measures a property nobody was measuring).

What the distribution means

Twenty-three papers modify the training objective; seven modify the action interface. That ratio — better than three to one — is the field’s revealed preference, and it is worth sitting with.

The interpretation I find most defensible: the objective layer is where you can make progress without owning a robot fleet or a pretraining budget. You take a released backbone, add a supervision signal, fine-tune, and evaluate. The action interface, by contrast, is a foundational design choice. Changing it properly means retraining from a much earlier stage, and validating it means demonstrating that the change survives scale — which requires exactly the resources most groups lack. The field is optimizing where it can afford to optimize.

This is not a criticism of any individual paper. It is a structural observation about what a research community does under a compute constraint, and it predicts something: the action-interface layer is under-explored relative to its importance, and is likely to be where a well-resourced group finds disproportionate returns. Seven papers is not many for a design decision that every VLA makes and that plausibly bounds what any of them can achieve.

The inference layer’s size (17 papers) tells a complementary story. Test-time intervention is the other place where a small group can contribute without pretraining, and the training-free framing that recurs across this cluster — SCALE, SpecPrune-VLA, EcoVLA, CAPS, VAP all advertise requiring no additional training — is a direct adaptation to resource scarcity. It is also, I think, genuinely good news: a training-free method that works on a frozen backbone is more likely to compose with future models than a method requiring bespoke fine-tuning.

04The clusters

Ten clusters carry the corpus. I have written each around the question a researcher actually needs answered: not what did these papers do, but where do they disagree with each other — because unresolved internal disagreement is where the openings are.

4.1 Latent action representations (15 papers)

The largest cluster, and the one most invisible in submission-side analyses of the same cycle.

The problem: robot action data is scarce and embodiment-specific, while video of things happening is abundant and embodiment-free. A latent action model tries to bridge these by inferring, from consecutive video frames alone, a compact code describing “what changed” — a pseudo-action label. Train that on internet video, and you can pretrain a policy on data that has no action labels at all.

The idea is older than this cycle. What is new is that the field has moved from does this work to what exactly should the latent encode, and the papers here disagree productively.

MVP-LAM identifies a specific failure: latent actions learned from single-view video absorb viewpoint-specific visual cues that have nothing to do with the underlying action. Its fix is a cross-viewpoint reconstruction objective — a latent inferred from one camera must explain the future as seen from a different camera, which forces out view-dependent information. The paper reports higher mutual information with ground-truth actions on Bridge V2, which is the right metric for the claim: it measures whether the latent is about the action rather than whether it happens to help downstream.

LARA attacks a different weakness: latent action models and VLAs are typically trained in separate stages, leaving the latent model ungrounded in real actions and the policy locked to a frozen representation. LARA optimizes both jointly, and reports gains across three regimes — pretraining, post-training enhancement of an already-trained VLA, and refinement of the latent model itself.

XR-1 (spotlight) goes further and argues the split between “visual dynamics” latents and “robot motion” latents is itself the mistake. Its Unified Vision-Motion Codes are a discrete latent from a dual-branch VQ-VAE with a shared codebook, encoding both. The validation is the most substantial in this cluster and one of the most substantial in the corpus: over 12,000 real-world rollouts across six embodiments and more than 120 tasks, reporting gains over both π₀ and GR00T-N1.5.

“From Pixels to Tokens” (spotlight) is the cluster’s own literature review and the paper I would hand to someone entering this area. It compares four integration strategies under a single unified baseline and reports a formulation-task correspondence: image-based latent actions help long-horizon reasoning, action-based latent actions help fine motor coordination, and directly supervising the vision-language model with discrete latent action tokens works best overall.

That last finding is the cluster’s most consequential claim and also its sharpest internal tension. It says the winning move is discrete token supervision — which sits uneasily beside the neighboring work arguing that discretization is the field’s core pathology (§4.2). Both positions have credible evidence. Nobody in this corpus reconciles them.

Elsewhere in the cluster: BehaviorVLA (spotlight) uses a causal Mamba encoder to aggregate long-horizon trajectory information into a behavior representation, with a phase-conditioned decoder that aligns task priors against real-time execution progress — an explicit attack on the temporal fragmentation that short-horizon latents suffer from. RS-CL takes the simplest approach in the cluster and is worth attention for that reason: a contrastive loss that aligns representations with proprioceptive state, using relative distances between states as soft supervision, reported to lift RoboCasa-Kitchen to 69.7% and real-robot success from 45.0% to 58.3%. UniJEPA brings joint-embedding predictive architectures into the picture, pretraining on over a million internet instructional manipulation videos. LangForce identifies “information collapse” — in goal-driven datasets, the instruction is predictable from the image, the conditional mutual information between instruction and action vanishes, and the policy quietly degenerates into a vision-only controller that ignores language. Its fix maximizes pointwise mutual information between actions and instructions, reporting an 11.3% gain on out-of-distribution SimplerEnv.

LangForce deserves emphasis beyond its cluster. If its diagnosis is right, a large fraction of reported language-following performance across the field is an artifact of datasets where language was never load-bearing. That is a claim about the validity of everyone else’s numbers, and it is the kind of finding that should reorganize a subfield.

Where the openings are. Nobody here evaluates latent action quality with a shared metric — MVP-LAM’s mutual-information framing is the most principled and is used by exactly one paper. Nobody tests whether latents learned from human video and latents learned from robot video are interchangeable. And nobody in this cluster connects to the memory cluster, despite both being about compressing temporal context.

4.2 Action interface and tokenization (8 papers)

Small, foundational, and split down the middle on a question the field has not settled: should actions be discrete?

The discrete camp. RoboOmni makes the strongest version of the argument, and its title is the thesis — actions are just another modality. It argues that unified discrete frameworks have lagged continuous ones only because of action-chunking and temporal-modeling limitations, not because discreteness is wrong, and fixes those with Multi-Token Action Prediction that folds chunking into the tokenizer. Reported results: 94.4% average on CALVIN with the FAST tokenizer, and a 27× inference speedup over OpenVLA with a simple bin tokenizer. That second number is the interesting one, because it reframes the debate as a Pareto question rather than an accuracy question.

Discrete Diffusion VLA takes the same side by a different route, keeping action generation inside the unified transformer backbone instead of attaching an external diffusion head. Its adaptive decoding resolves confident action elements first and re-masks uncertain ones for revision. The reported evidence I find most compelling is not the headline success rate (96.4% average on LIBERO, 71.2% SimplerEnv-Fractal visual matching, 54.2% SimplerEnv-Bridge) but the degradation analysis: 0.8% language degradation on out-of-distribution LIBERO-Goal versus 8.0% for parallel decoding, and 20.4% vision degradation versus 29.0% for continuous diffusion. Those are measurements of whether pretrained vision-language capability survives the conversion to a policy, which is the thing the whole VLA premise rests on and which success rates do not measure.

The continuous camp. NIAF argues the discretization is an artifact of how we collect data and how language models decode, not a property of motion — fixed-rate sampling and token-by-token prediction, neither of which physical motion cares about. It replaces chunk-level waypoints with a continuous action function, using the vision-language model as a hierarchical spectral modulator over a learnable motion prior. The payoff is that continuous functions are analytically differentiable: you can supervise velocity directly, regularize higher-order derivatives, and get smoothness guarantees that waypoint prediction cannot provide. The paper reports that this supports stable impedance control on real hardware, which is the practical argument — quantization artifacts are invisible in success-rate benchmarks and very visible in contact-rich control.

GAM makes a related structural argument: regressing absolute coordinates violates general covariance, conflating task geometry with execution style and binding policies to specific speeds and motion patterns. Its framework enforces temporal invariance via arc-length parameterization (decoupling path geometry from timing) and geometric invariance via mapping trajectories to canonical world lines in a Lie-algebraic tangent space. LAST arrives at Lie algebra from the opposite direction — it frames VLA learning as a Gromov-Wasserstein alignment between a topologically linear, isotropic semantic space and a non-Euclidean, anisotropic action manifold, then linearizes the action manifold so the metrics become compatible.

That two independent papers converge on Lie-algebraic action representations is the cluster’s most interesting signal. It suggests the field is discovering that the action space has geometric structure that flat regression and naive binning both destroy.

ResVLA occupies a middle position worth noting: rather than generating actions from noise, it decomposes motion spectrally into a deterministic low-frequency intent anchor and a stochastic high-frequency residual, then uses a diffusion bridge to refine only the residual. This is a partial concession from both sides — structure where structure exists, generative modeling only where it is needed.

Where the openings are. No paper here evaluates the discrete-versus-continuous question directly under controlled conditions. RoboOmni and NIAF are arguing past each other on different benchmarks with different backbones. A rigorous head-to-head — same backbone, same data, same tasks, varying only the action interface, measured on both success rate and control smoothness — does not exist in this corpus and would be immediately valuable.

4.3 World models and video prediction (11 papers)

Reuss’s ICLR analysis noted that video-prediction policies were far less popular than VLM-initialized ones and expressed hope for more work. In this corpus that hope is met — but the papers have moved past “use video prediction as a pretraining objective” into three distinct and often-conflated uses of a world model.

World model as auxiliary supervision. WoG compresses future observations into compact conditions injected into the action inference pipeline, training the policy to predict those conditions alongside actions — explicitly trading pixel-level fidelity for a representation that is predictable and still fine-grained enough to guide control. DUST keeps separate modality streams in a multimodal diffusion transformer with independent noise perturbations and a decoupled flow-matching loss, reporting up to 6% gains on RoboCasa and GR-1 plus 2–5% more from inference-time scaling. FOCA predicts task-grounded future interaction embeddings and aligns implicitly to future goal observations, deliberately avoiding pixel-level prediction, and frames the result as learning a future-conditioned value-like representation — reporting 95.7% on LIBERO with 20 demonstrations.

World model as training environment. VLA-MBPO confronts the practical obstacles to model-based RL for VLAs head-on: pixel-level world modeling cost, multi-view consistency, and compounding error under sparse reward. Its answers — adapting unified multimodal models for data-efficient world modeling, interleaved view decoding, chunk-level branched rollout — are the most systems-aware treatment of world-model RL in the corpus, and its claim to maintain a universal hyperparameter set across tasks is the kind of robustness claim that matters more than a headline number.

VLAW is the cluster’s most honest paper. It states directly that existing world models lack the physical fidelity needed for policy improvement, because they are trained on demonstration data that contains almost no failures and cannot model the small contact details that determine manipulation outcomes. Its response is an iterative loop: use real rollouts to improve world-model fidelity, then use the improved model to generate synthetic data for the policy. The reported decomposition is unusually informative — 39.2% absolute improvement over the base policy total, of which 11.6% came from the synthetic rollouts. That second number is the one to internalize. The world model contributed real value, and it contributed less than a third of the gain.

World model as optimization target. Dyn-VPP observes that likelihood-trained diffusion predictors optimize for globally plausible futures, which is not the same as the precision-critical dynamics manipulation needs, and small prediction errors get amplified downstream. It casts multi-step denoising as policy optimization with a verifiable terminal reward, without architectural changes.

Where the openings are. The cluster shares an unexamined assumption: that better future prediction produces better policies. VLAW’s decomposition is the only evidence in the corpus that quantifies how much of a gain is actually attributable to the world model, and the answer was “less than you would guess.” A study that varies world-model fidelity systematically and measures downstream policy quality would tell the field whether it is investing in the right place. Related: every paper here predicts forward. Nobody uses the world model to reason counterfactually about what would have happened under a different action, which is the thing world models are supposed to be uniquely good for.

4.4 Reasoning and chain-of-thought (12 papers)

This cluster contains the corpus’s clearest live argument, and it is one a researcher can act on immediately.

Embodied chain-of-thought has an obvious cost: generating reasoning tokens autoregressively before every action is slow, and robot control is rate-constrained. The cluster splits on what to do about that.

Move reasoning into latent space. LaST₀ (spotlight) proposes a latent spatio-temporal chain-of-thought that models future visual dynamics, 3D structure, and proprioceptive state in a token-efficient latent space, arguing that linguistic reasoning is not just slow but representationally bottlenecked — physical attributes that matter for manipulation are difficult to verbalize at all. Its dual-system Mixture-of-Transformers runs a reasoning expert at low frequency and an acting expert at high frequency, trained with heterogeneous operating frequencies so the system can switch adaptively. Reported: 13%, 14%, and 14% mean success improvements across tabletop, mobile, and dexterous-hand manipulation over ten real-world tasks.

LaRA-VLA internalizes multimodal chain-of-thought into continuous latents through a curriculum that starts with explicit textual and visual supervision and progressively transitions to latent reasoning, reporting up to 90% reduction in inference latency versus explicit CoT. AVA-VLA models reasoning as unobservable latent variables, adds RL-based denoising of latent trajectories against task-level rewards, and an early-exit strategy that terminates reasoning based on state confidence.

Keep reasoning explicit, and scale it. HALO argues the opposite: that neither textual chain-of-thought nor visual subgoal prediction alone gives a unified framework, and builds embodied multimodal CoT combining textual reasoning, fine-grained visual subgoal prediction, and CoT-augmented action prediction — with a Mixture-of-Transformers separating semantic reasoning, visual foresight, and action into specialized experts. Reported: 25.9% over π₀ on RoboTwin, with explicit generalization gains under aggressive environment randomization.

Reason only when necessary. A third position, arguably the most practical. Sentinel-VLA adds a monitoring module that triggers reasoning only during initial planning or upon detecting an error, keeping the fast path fast. NeurVLA takes a neural-symbolic route to both failure correction and prevention. SCALE (spotlight) modulates both perception and action based on self-uncertainty, requiring no additional training, no verifier, and a single forward pass.

The three positions are genuinely incompatible and the evidence does not adjudicate between them, because each is evaluated on different tasks against different baselines. My read is that the disagreement is partly definitional: latent reasoning and explicit reasoning are being asked to do different jobs. Latent methods target continuous physical inference — dynamics, geometry, contact — that language represents poorly. Explicit methods target compositional task decomposition, which language represents well and latents represent poorly. If that framing is right, the answer is not one or the other but a division of labor along the axis of what is verbalizable, and no paper in this corpus tests it.

A caution. TRAP demonstrates that CoT reasoning creates an attack surface: an adversarial patch (a printed tablecloth, in their real-world implementation) can hijack the reasoning-to-action pathway and induce targeted behaviors — their example is redirecting a robot to hand a person a knife instead of an apple — without modifying the user’s instruction. Their supporting finding is the one that should worry the cluster: CoT strongly governs action generation even when it is semantically misaligned with the input instruction. Interpretability and controllability are the standard arguments for reasoning traces. TRAP shows the same causal pathway is a control surface for whoever can influence the scene.

4.5 Efficiency and deployment (15 papers)

The second-largest cluster by any-mention, and the one where this corpus most clearly diverges from a submission-pile picture of the field. It splits cleanly by what gets cut.

Visual tokens. GridS reframes compression as geometry-aware continuous resampling rather than discrete pruning, arguing that aggressive token pruning necessarily discards contact points and other fine geometric detail. It predicts a minimal set of salient coordinates and extracts features by differentiable interpolation, reporting 76% FLOPs reduction with under 10% of original visual tokens and no success-rate degradation. SpecPrune-VLA exploits spatial-temporal consistency — consecutive observations are highly similar — combining action-level static pruning using global history with layer-level dynamic pruning, plus a controller that classifies actions as coarse or fine by end-effector speed and adjusts pruning aggressiveness accordingly. Reported: 1.57× in LIBERO simulation, 1.70× on real tasks.

Model parameters. EcoVLA (spotlight) observes that optimal sparsity patterns change as the environment evolves during execution, making static pruning inadequate and fixed-interval dynamic pruning too coarse. Its adaptive channel pruning uses temporal consistency of the physical environment to update sparsity patterns, and its interleaved orchestration hides the pruning computation in FLOPs bubbles already present in VLA inference. Reported: 1.60× speedup at 0.4% success drop, 2.18× at 0.5% when composed with token pruning. That composability claim is worth more than the raw numbers — it means the acceleration techniques in this cluster may stack.

The inference loop itself. Reflex is the most technically specific paper in the cluster and identifies a real incompatibility: flow-matching policies inject a global timestep, which invalidates KV-caching, forcing a choice between O(N²) recomputation and mathematically incorrect cache reuse. Its insight is that perception encoders are functionally independent of the denoising loop, which permits partitioning attention context into static, sliding, and dynamic regions and doing O(1) incremental cache updates while preserving exact attention outputs. It also reports needing an adaptive normalization layer to prevent BFloat16 numerical collapse under continuous high-frequency inference — the kind of detail that only surfaces when someone actually runs a policy at 50Hz for a long time. Reported: 2.58× speedup, stable 50Hz streaming, up to 54% reaction-latency reduction.

The hardware. “Characterizing VLA Models across XPUs” is the paper I would most like the community to read, because it asks a question nobody else asks: what does this cost on hardware a robot can actually carry? It evaluates model-hardware pairs under cost, energy, and time jointly, and reports that right-sized edge devices can beat flagship GPUs on cost and energy while still meeting control-rate constraints. Its diagnostic finding, from SM tracing and roofline analysis, is a consistent two-phase inference pattern — a compute-bound VLM backbone followed by a memory-bound action expert — which induces phase-dependent underutilization on any single accelerator. That is a structural observation about VLA architecture disguised as a systems paper, and it implies the current architecture is close to worst-case for hardware utilization.

SpikeVLA is the cluster’s outlier: an end-to-end spiking neural network VLA for navigation, replacing dense continuous computation with event-driven spiking representations across encoder, language model, and action policy. Whether or not spiking hardware arrives, it is the only paper in the corpus taking the energy budget as a first-class constraint rather than an afterthought.

Where the openings are. Almost every paper reports speedup against a success-rate delta, but there is no shared Pareto frame — no paper plots the full accuracy-latency-energy frontier across methods, and no paper reports whether these methods compose beyond EcoVLA’s single pairing. Given the composability claim, an empirical study of how the acceleration stack interacts would be immediately useful and requires no new method.

4.6 Test-time compute, critics, and composition (7 papers)

The field is importing the test-time-scaling playbook from language models, and running into a problem language models do not have: robots cannot resample after acting.

VLA-ATTC frames VLA decision-making as reflexive and lacking deliberation, adding an uncertainty-based trigger that transitions to a deliberation phase when needed. Its contribution is the relative action critic: identifying the best action from candidates by pairwise comparison rather than absolute value estimation, on the argument that relative judgments are a much easier learning target. Reported: over 50% failure-rate reduction on LIBERO-LONG relative to π₀.₅. VLAC builds a generalist critic that unifies action policy and progress critic in one autoregressive architecture, predicting the delta of task progress between two steps — again a relative formulation — and using that as a dense intrinsic reward for real-world RL.

The convergence of two independent papers on relative rather than absolute value estimation is a signal worth taking seriously. Absolute value estimation in manipulation requires a reward model that knows what success looks like in every context; relative comparison requires only that it can rank two attempts. The second is a far weaker requirement.

SCALE (spotlight) takes the opposite tack on cost: it argues existing test-time-scaling methods for VLAs require extra training, verifiers, and multiple forward passes, making them undeployable, and that intervening only at action decoding is insufficient when the problem is perceptual ambiguity — reconsidering how to perceive matters as much as deciding what to do. Its inference strategy jointly modulates perception and action based on self-uncertainty, in a single forward pass with no training.

CAPS reconceptualizes instruction drift in long-horizon tasks as a sampling error: greedy local sampling collapses into “negative pivotal windows,” locally high-probability states that sever the path to global success. Its training-free fix uses power distributions to sharpen global trajectory probabilities and enables lookahead search, with an SNR-based trigger for adaptive MCMC search only when drift risk is detected. EnsembleVLA establishes that diffusion-based and flow-based VLAs can both be formulated as energy-based models, so additive energy combination induces principled policy composition at the distribution level — the same theoretical move that lets multiple pretrained policies be aggregated without retraining.

Where the openings are. Every method here is evaluated on when it helps. None reports how often the deliberation trigger fires spuriously, or what the deliberation costs when it fires and does not help. For a rate-constrained system, a method that triggers on 40% of steps to fix 5% of failures may be net negative, and the corpus gives no way to tell.

4.7 Robustness and distribution shift (11 papers)

LIBERO-Gen is the cluster’s anchor and makes the sharpest methodological claim in the corpus: current evaluation protocols incentivize memorization, producing a paradoxical failure pattern where high-scoring models are simultaneously spuriously invariant to semantic changes and extremely brittle to trivial environmental perturbations. Its hierarchical protocol separates in-distribution, compositional, and domain generalization. The numbers it reports are the corpus’s most sobering: the top performer, π₀.₅, reaches 64.0% on spatial compositional generalization and 21.2% on task compositional generalization. Against LIBERO’s headline scores of 95%+, that is the gap between benchmark competence and capability.

StableVLA studies robustness to unseen visual disturbances and reports a significant drop across state-of-the-art models. Its information-bottleneck adapter improves over baseline by an average of 30% with under 10M added parameters and no extra data. The result I find most striking is a scaling one: a 0.5B-parameter backbone with no Open X-Embodiment pretraining reaches robustness competitive with 7B-scale models. If that holds, a meaningful fraction of what large VLAs buy is not robustness.

“Pretrained VLAs are Surprisingly Resistant to Forgetting” (spotlight) is a pure empirical study and one of the corpus’s most useful papers. It finds that large pretrained VLAs resist catastrophic forgetting far better than small policies trained from scratch, that simple experience replay sometimes achieves zero forgetting with a small buffer, and — most interestingly — that apparent forgetting is often not forgetting at all: knowledge is retained despite performance degradation, and seemingly lost skills recover rapidly under fine-tuning. That reframes continual learning for VLAs as a retrieval problem rather than a retention problem.

Embodied Interpretability formulates visual-action attribution as interventional estimation, introducing an Interventional Significance Score for the causal influence of visual regions and a Nuisance Mass Ratio measuring attribution to task-irrelevant features — and reports that NMR predicts generalization behavior. A cheap diagnostic that predicts generalization without running the generalization test would be a valuable tool if it holds up.

TAP offers the cluster’s most striking robustness number almost in passing: under camera perturbations, 25% versus 0% for internet-scale baselines. A baseline scoring zero under a camera shift is a more informative datapoint about the state of VLA robustness than most benchmark tables.

4.8 Data: generation, curation, and the human-video route (7 + 7 papers)

Reuss identified data quality as the field’s most under-addressed problem. This corpus partially contradicts that — there is real data work here — but the kind of data work is narrow.

RoboTwin 2.0 is the largest infrastructure contribution: a scalable generator built on a library of 731 object instances across 147 categories, integrating multimodal LLMs with simulation-in-the-loop verification to automatically fix failed task programs, plus structured domain randomization across clutter, lighting, background, tabletop height, and language. Instantiated across 50 dual-arm tasks and five embodiments, it reports 3.6× improvement in few-shot real-world transfer over a 10-demo baseline and 2.2× in zero-shot generalization.

RDT2 takes the scale route: over 10,000 hours of demonstrations collected with an embodiment-agnostic Universal Manipulation Interface, on a 7B backbone, with a three-stage recipe combining residual vector quantization, flow matching, and distillation for real-time inference — targeting zero-shot deployment on unseen embodiments, objects, scenes, and instructions.

“Seeing Realism from Simulation” converts simulated videos into realistic ones while preserving task semantics and action trajectories, with a diffusion feature-reuse mechanism for speed and coreset sampling to pick a compact augmentation subset. Reported: RDT-1B improved 8% on RoboTwin 2.0, π₀ improved 5.1% on LIBERO-Plus.

Anchor-Centric Adaptation is the only paper in the corpus that studies data collection strategy as an object of theory, and it is the one I would most encourage people to read. It identifies a “diversity trap”: the standard heuristic of maximizing coverage by collecting diverse single-shot demonstrations can be self-defeating, because estimation noise does not vanish. It formalizes this as a coverage-density trade-off, decomposes policy error into estimation and extrapolation terms, and derives an interior optimal allocation of unique conditions under a fixed budget — then proposes stabilizing a policy skeleton with repeated demonstrations at core anchors before expanding coverage to high-risk boundaries.

That is a genuinely different kind of contribution. Everyone in robot learning collects demonstrations under a budget, and the field’s operative heuristic is “collect diverse data.” This paper argues the heuristic is wrong at the margin and says where the optimum is.

On the human-video side, Being-H0 treats human hands as a manipulator template, with physical instruction tuning combining large-scale pretraining from human video, perspective spatial alignment, and post-training adaptation — plus part-level motion tokenization at millimeter reconstruction accuracy. It is one of very few papers in the corpus reporting favorable scaling behavior with respect to model and data size. TAP decouples learning how to move from learning what to do, pretraining on discarded off-task trajectories and autonomous robot play with an inverse dynamics objective, then aligning to language with minimal expert data — reporting that it matches models trained on a million-plus expert trajectories on SIMPLER.

What is still missing. All of this is data generation and acquisition. Almost nothing addresses data quality assessment: no method for scoring an existing demonstration, no method for identifying which trajectories in a large corpus are harmful to train on, no metric for demonstration quality that does not require training a policy to find out. Open X-Embodiment’s uneven quality is widely acknowledged and this corpus contains no tool for measuring it.

4.9 Memory and long-horizon control (4 papers)

Small but coherent, and containing the corpus’s best-designed benchmark.

RoboMME (spotlight) is a memory benchmark built on a deliberate taxonomy — temporal, spatial, object, and procedural memory — with 16 tasks, and it does something rare: it also builds 14 memory-augmented VLA variants on a π₀.₅ backbone to explore memory representations across integration strategies. Its headline finding is negative and valuable: memory representation effectiveness is highly task-dependent, with each design showing distinct advantages and limitations. No memory architecture dominates.

SOMA attacks a specific and under-noticed assumption: VLAs implicitly assume task-relevant objects are visible, and behave reactively and brittlely when targets fall outside the camera’s field of view. It builds persistent spatial memory from multi-view observations via a movable head camera, refines it for global consistency, and retrieves instruction-relevant cues during manipulation. What makes it interesting is the reported qualitative change: faster target localization, reduced viewpoint search, near one-shot grasping under partial observability. The policy does not just succeed more; it behaves differently.

HiMe names the cluster’s central tension well — a “frequency-competence paradox” where high-performance models are too slow for real-time control while fast models cannot reason — and decouples execution into a high-frequency executor, a working-memory sentry, and a long-term planner, with add/update/delete operations over a semantic knowledge store.

Where the openings are. RoboMME establishes that no memory design generalizes, which is the field’s cue to figure out why — task-conditional memory selection is an obvious follow-up that nobody in this corpus attempts. And the memory cluster and the latent-representation cluster are solving overlapping problems (compressing temporal context into a useful state) with zero cross-citation visible in the abstracts.

4.10 Beyond tabletop manipulation (11 papers)

The corpus’s quiet expansion. Four driving papers, two navigation, one aerial, one tactile.

Driving is the most developed. DynVLA proposes “Dynamics CoT” — forecasting compact world dynamics before action, with a tokenizer compressing future evolution into a small set of dynamics tokens and a decoupling of ego-centric from environment-centric dynamics — arguing explicitly against both textual CoT (no fine-grained spatiotemporal understanding) and visual CoT (redundant dense image prediction). DriveWorld-VLA unifies world modeling and planning in latent space, using world-model latent states as the planner’s decision states and avoiding pixel-level rollouts. AutoMoT uses layer-wise joint attention sharing in a Mixture-of-Transformers to preserve pretrained VLM reasoning while running tasks asynchronously at different frequencies, and — unusually — investigates the functional boundary of pretrained VLMs for driving, asking when domain-specific fine-tuning is actually necessary.

The driving subfield is ahead of manipulation on one axis: it has closed-loop evaluation protocols (NAVSIM, Bench2Drive) that manipulation lacks, and papers report both open-loop and closed-loop results as a matter of course.

TIC-VLA contributes the cluster’s most transferable idea. It observes that VLAs assume temporally aligned reasoning and control, when semantic inference is inherently delayed relative to real-time action. Rather than trying to make reasoning fast enough, it conditions action generation on delayed semantic states plus explicit latency metadata, and injects realistic reasoning delays during training so training matches asynchronous deployment. Treating latency as a modeled variable rather than an engineering defect is the right frame, and it applies well beyond navigation.

AIR-VLA extends VLA to aerial manipulation, where floating-base dynamics and UAV-manipulator coupling break assumptions built for static or ground-mobile bases, releasing a simulator and 3,000 teleoperated demonstrations. Tabero addresses tactile sensing with a decoupled force-position command interface executed by a fixed hybrid controller, and evaluates on physical interaction quality alongside task success — reporting over 70% reduction in average grip force under gentle instructions while maintaining success. That evaluation choice deserves imitation. Success rate cannot distinguish a robot that picks up an egg from one that crushes it, and a large fraction of real manipulation requirements are of this kind.

05The evaluation picture

The saturation story is confirmed. LIBERO appears in 16 papers, more than twice any other benchmark, and papers report averages in the 95–98% range as a matter of course. Discrete Diffusion VLA reports 96.4%; BehaviorVLA reports 98%. At that level, ranking is noise. LIBERO-Gen’s stratified re-evaluation puts a number on the illusion: π₀.₅, the strongest model tested, drops to 21.2% on task compositional generalization.

CALVIN has been effectively abandoned — three papers, down from a position as a core benchmark. RoboTwin (5) and RoboCasa (4) are rising, which is a healthy shift toward bimanual and kitchen-scale settings with more room above the ceiling. SIMPLER’s 7 appearances come with the known caveat that its numbers are not comparable across setups.

Real-robot evaluation is the norm, not the exception. By my coding, 45 of 69 papers (65%) present substantive real-robot evidence — a named platform, task counts, or reported success numbers, not just the phrase “real-world.” The 24 that do not are largely papers where it would be strange: driving papers evaluated on driving benchmarks, the hardware characterization study, the continual-learning analysis, several theory-forward action-interface papers. This is a meaningfully different picture from the submission-side complaint that VLA evaluation is sim-only, and it is the strongest evidence in this analysis that peer review is selecting for something real.

The caveat: I cannot verify quality from abstracts. “Real-world experiments” covers everything from XR-1’s 12,000 rollouts across six embodiments to a handful of demonstrations on one arm. The distribution within that 65% is unknown and probably wide.

And then the proliferation problem. Ten of 69 papers introduce a new evaluation artifact:

Artifact Contribution
VLA-Arena 170 tasks, 11 suites, orthogonal difficulty axes for structure, language, vision
RoboMME Memory benchmark on a four-way taxonomy, plus 14 memory-augmented baselines
RoboTwin 2.0 Data generator + benchmark, 50 dual-arm tasks, five embodiments
LIBERO-Gen Diagnostic re-stratification of LIBERO by generalization type
VLA-FixBench Fault taxonomy across perception, planning, and control failures
AIR-VLA Aerial manipulation simulator + 3,000 demonstrations

LIBERO-LaRA / Bridge-LaRA Structured chain-of-thought annotation datasets

Individually, most are good. VLA-Arena’s design in particular is careful — three orthogonal difficulty axes, fine-tuning restricted to the easiest level to force generalization, and a reported validation that model rankings reverse across difficulty levels, which is direct evidence that each level carries non-redundant information.

Collectively, they are a problem. The field diagnosed benchmark saturation and responded by fragmenting. A researcher in 2027 comparing two methods will face a choice among ten incompatible suites, none of which has enough adoption to make its numbers meaningful, and each of which was introduced alongside a method that performs well on it. The incentive structure here is bad in a familiar way: introducing a benchmark with your method is a reliable path to a favorable comparison.

The fix is not fewer benchmarks. It is that new benchmarks need adoption commitments — third-party evaluation, standing leaderboards, and results reported for models the benchmark’s authors did not train. RoboMME’s 14 baseline variants and VLA-Arena’s public leaderboard are the right instincts. They should be the minimum bar.

06Baseline archaeology: who the field measures against

Which models a corpus compares against reveals what it considers the state of the art, and the answer has shifted.

π₀ and π₀.₅ appear in 7 papers. OpenVLA appears in 3. GR00T in 1. RDT in 1. Octo in none.

Physical Intelligence’s π₀ family has become the field’s default reference policy. This is a real change from the OpenVLA-centric period, and it has a straightforward cause: π₀ is strong enough that beating it is a meaningful claim, and open enough to actually run. Where OpenVLA appears, it is increasingly as the efficiency baseline — RoboOmni’s 27× speedup is measured against OpenVLA, and CAPS uses it as a starting policy — rather than as the accuracy target.

Two consequences worth naming. First, the field’s sense of “state of the art” is now anchored to a model from a single company, and the community’s evaluation of progress is partly a function of what that company chooses to release. Second, the papers reporting the largest margins over π₀ — HALO’s 25.9% on RoboTwin, VLA-ATTC’s 50% failure-rate reduction on LIBERO-LONG — are reporting them on simulation benchmarks, in exactly the regime where LIBERO-Gen showed that benchmark performance and generalization come apart. XR-1 is the notable exception: its comparison against π₀ and GR00T-N1.5 runs on 12,000 real rollouts across six embodiments.

The general lesson: margins over π₀ in simulation and margins over π₀ on hardware are different claims, and the corpus contains many more of the first than the second.

07Gaps

I have sorted these by how confident I am that the gap is real, rather than an artifact of a keyword-scoped, single-venue, abstract-only corpus.

Confidently real

Safety and adversarial robustness: one paper, and it is an attack. TRAP is the entire security footprint of 69 papers — 1.4% — and it demonstrates a working real-world attack without proposing a defense. Meanwhile the corpus is full of work on deploying these systems on physical hardware near people. VLA-Arena’s evaluation includes a safety axis and reports that current models show “a neglect of safety constraints.” Nobody is working on that finding. This is the corpus’s most alarming gap, because the deployment work and the safety work are diverging rather than converging.

Interpretability: one paper. Embodied Interpretability is alone, which is odd given that a standard argument for chain-of-thought VLAs is interpretability. The field claims interpretability as a benefit and does almost no work on measuring it. TRAP’s finding — that CoT drives action even when semantically misaligned with the instruction — suggests reasoning traces may be less faithful than assumed, which makes the absence more concerning rather than less.

Data quality assessment. Data generation is well represented; data evaluation is not. There is no method in this corpus for scoring an existing demonstration, identifying harmful trajectories in a large corpus, or predicting a dataset’s downstream value without training on it. Anchor-Centric Adaptation is the closest thing, and it addresses collection strategy rather than assessment of data already collected.

Scaling laws. Being-H0 mentions favorable scaling with model and data size; that is essentially the corpus’s entire treatment. Nobody characterizes how VLA performance scales with pretraining data, model size, or embodiment diversity. Every architectural comparison in the corpus is therefore made at a single scale, with no way to know whether the ranking survives scaling — which is precisely the failure mode that made pre-scaling-law NLP results unreliable.

Negative results and failed approaches. Not one paper reports an idea that did not work. Reuss asked for pretraining recipes that document failures; this corpus does not deliver them, and the two systematic studies that come closest (VLANeXt’s 12 findings, the latent-action supervision study) frame their results as recipes rather than as post-mortems.

Probably real, with caveats

In-context learning. RA-VLA is the single paper, using retrieval-augmented context with behavior-aligned retrieval for training-free adaptation. Given how central in-context learning is to how language models are actually used, one paper is remarkably few. The caveat: in-context imitation learning has a literature that may not use VLA vocabulary, so the keyword scope could be hiding work here.

Dexterity. Three papers touch dexterous manipulation (LaST₀’s dexterous-hand tasks, RDT2, Being-H0’s hand-motion modeling). Nearly all evaluation in the corpus is parallel-jaw gripper manipulation. Being-H0’s premise — that human hands are the right manipulator template because web video contains enormous dexterous data — is compelling and almost unexplored.

Tactile and force. One paper. Tabero’s own framing explains why: aligned vision-tactile-language data barely exists. This is a data bottleneck, not a lack of interest, and Tabero’s approach of repurposing existing manipulation trajectories to generate tactile tasks is a template others could follow.

Multi-robot and human-robot interaction. Zero papers on either. Every policy in this corpus controls one robot in isolation, with humans appearing only as demonstration sources.

Possibly artifacts of scope

RL fine-tuning appears weaker than it is. Seven papers use RL, but none as the central contribution in the way Reuss’s ICLR analysis described, and — notably — there is not a single mention of GRPO or DPO anywhere in the corpus. The VLA field has not imported the post-training playbook that reorganized language-model fine-tuning. Whether that is because it does not transfer, because the reward-modeling problem is harder, or because this venue’s reviewers were unconvinced, the data cannot say. VLAC’s progress critic and VLA-ATTC’s relative action critic are both, in effect, attempts to build the missing reward model.

Discrete diffusion. One paper — and see §9, because the story is more interesting than the count.

3D representations. Any3D-VLA is essentially alone in the corpus, despite 3D policy methods substantially outperforming VLAs on 3D benchmarks. Reuss noted that VLA papers tend to avoid comparing against strong 3D baselines. This corpus is consistent with that, though the 3D policy literature largely does not self-identify as VLA work, so the scope caveat applies with full force.

08Opinion: where I would place bets

This section is my judgment rather than an account of the data. It is separated deliberately; everything above stands without it.

The action interface is the most underpriced problem in the corpus. Seven papers on a design decision that every VLA makes, that plausibly bounds control quality, and where two independent groups converged on Lie-algebraic structure without knowing about each other. The reason it is underpriced is structural — validating an action-interface change requires retraining at scale, which most groups cannot do — which means it is exactly where a well-resourced group has an advantage that compounds. The specific experiment I would run first is the one nobody ran: same backbone, same data, same tasks, vary only the action interface, and measure both success rate and trajectory smoothness under contact.

LangForce’s information collapse should be treated as a validity crisis, not a paper. If instructions are predictable from observations in standard datasets, then conditional mutual information between language and action vanishes and policies degenerate into vision-only controllers — and a large fraction of reported language-following results across the field measure something other than language following. The immediate work is not a better method. It is an audit: measure conditional mutual information between instructions and actions across the standard datasets and report which benchmarks can support language-grounding claims at all.

The reasoning debate has a resolvable structure and nobody is resolving it. Latent-CoT and explicit-CoT camps are evaluated on different tasks with different backbones, which makes the disagreement look deeper than it is. My hypothesis is a division of labor along verbalizability: explicit language for compositional task decomposition, latent representations for continuous physical inference. That hypothesis is directly testable by taking a single backbone, routing reasoning through either channel, and measuring performance separately on compositional versus contact-precision tasks. It would settle a live argument for the cost of one careful experiment.

Benchmark introductions need an adoption norm, and the community can impose one. The proliferation problem is a coordination failure, not a technical one. A norm — a new benchmark reports results for at least N models its authors did not train, and maintains a leaderboard open to third-party submission — would be enforceable by reviewers starting next cycle. RoboMME’s 14 baselines and VLA-Arena’s public leaderboard show the bar is achievable.

Someone should build the accuracy-latency-energy Pareto frontier. The efficiency cluster has produced a dozen acceleration techniques with individually credible numbers, one claim that they compose, and no shared frame for comparing them. The XPU characterization paper’s two-phase inference finding — compute-bound backbone, memory-bound action expert — suggests the architecture itself is close to worst-case for hardware utilization. A study combining that hardware analysis with a systematic composition of the acceleration stack would be high-value and requires no new method.

The one I am least sure about, and would most like to be wrong on: I do not think this corpus contains a result that moves the zero-shot open-world gap Reuss identified. There is excellent work here on robustness to perturbations, on generalization to compositional variants, and on transfer across embodiments. But the thing frontier models demonstrate — arbitrary objects, paraphrased instructions, unfamiliar rooms, no fine-tuning — is measured by almost nothing in this corpus. RDT2 and XR-1 target it most directly. LIBERO-Gen measures the gap without closing it. The rest of the field is improving a different quantity.

09Two views of one cycle: ICLR submissions and ICML accepts

The most useful thing about having both pictures is that they overlap, and the overlap is checkable.

At least two papers appear in both. Discrete Diffusion VLA and XR-1 were both highlighted in Reuss’s ICLR 2026 submission analysis and both appear in this ICML accept list. RoboTwin 2.0 appears here as a paper and there as cited infrastructure. That is a documented resubmission trail, and it changes how the comparison should be read: these are not two independent samples of the field. They are partly the same work, observed at two points in its review journey.

This immediately complicates the tempting reading of the discrete-diffusion numbers. Four concurrent discrete-diffusion papers appeared in the ICLR submission pile; one discrete-diffusion paper appears in this accept list — and it is one of those four. The naive conclusion, that the trend died in review, is not supported. The better description is consolidation: a crowded concurrent race resolved to a single surviving entry. Whether the other three were rejected, withdrawn, are still under review elsewhere, or landed at a venue outside this scrape, the data cannot say. This is the censored-data limitation from §1 doing real work, and it should temper every comparative claim below.

With that caveat stated, the divergences:

Theme ICLR 2026 submissions ICML 2026 accepts
Latent action representations Barely noted 15 papers — the largest cluster
World models / video prediction “far less popular, I hope to see more” 11 papers
Efficiency and deployment 2 highlighted papers 15 papers
Memory 1 highlighted paper 4 papers + a dedicated benchmark
Discrete diffusion 4 concurrent papers, headline trend 1 — the same paper
Action tokenizers Dedicated trend section 8, none framed as tokenizer papers
CALVIN Core benchmark 3 papers
Real-robot evaluation Described as rare 45 of 69
Data curation Named the top gap 7 papers
In-context learning Named as absent 1 paper

Three patterns stand out.

The gaps Reuss identified got partially filled — by the accept set, in the same cycle. He called out video-prediction policies as under-explored and hoped for more; there are 11 here. He called out memory as a major limitation; there are four plus a benchmark. He called out data curation as the field’s most crucial unsolved problem; there are seven, including one that treats collection strategy as a formal optimization. Either the field responded faster than a single cycle allows, or — much more likely — the submission-pile view systematically undercounts work that has not yet cleared review, and both venues were seeing the same emerging shift. The second reading suggests submission-pile snapshots lag the field slightly, in a specific direction: they overweight loud, easily-labeled trends and underweight quieter foundational work.

Real-robot evaluation is the sharpest divergence, and the most likely to be causal. The submission-side complaint is that VLA evaluation is sim-only and untrustworthy. Sixty-five percent of these accepted papers present substantive real-robot evidence. I do not think that is a coincidence of venue. The most parsimonious explanation is that reviewers ask for hardware results and papers without them do not clear the bar. If so, the peer-review incentive problem is less severe than the submission-side view suggests — reviewers are asking for the right thing, even if they are not yet asking for open-world zero-shot evaluation specifically.

Efficiency is the other large divergence, and it points somewhere different. Two highlighted papers versus fifteen. The efficiency work here is not incremental; it includes a full hardware characterization across edge accelerators, a spiking-network VLA, and a streaming-inference framework that solves a real cache-invalidation problem in flow-matching policies. My reading is that the field has moved past treating deployment cost as an engineering afterthought, and that ICML’s reviewer population — more systems-literate than a pure robotics venue — rewards it.

What this comparison cannot establish. ICLR and ICML have different reviewer pools and different taste. Some of the divergence is venue, not time. A clean version of this analysis would compare submissions and accepts at the same venue, and that data will exist for ICLR 2026 once decisions are public. Until then, the resubmission trail is the most reliable thread: papers that appear in both sets are the ones where we can actually watch review act.

10Summary

Sixty-nine accepted papers describe a field in a different position than its own discourse suggests.

The discourse says VLA evaluation is sim-only; two-thirds of these papers present substantive real-robot results. The discourse says benchmarks are saturated and need replacing; the field responded by building ten incompatible replacements. The discourse says data quality is the top unaddressed problem; seven papers address data, but essentially none address quality as distinct from quantity. The discourse foregrounds discrete diffusion, tokenizers, and embodied chain-of-thought; the accept set is dominated by latent representations, world models, and inference-time efficiency.

The single structural fact I would carry away is the intervention distribution: 23 papers modify the training objective, 7 modify the action interface. The field is concentrated where progress is affordable rather than where it is most needed, which is exactly what a compute-constrained research community should be expected to do — and exactly why the action interface, and the pretraining recipes that would let anyone validate a change to it, remain the places where a well-resourced group can still find disproportionate returns.

And the gap Reuss named is still open. Nothing in this corpus demonstrates robust zero-shot behavior in unfamiliar environments with paraphrased instructions and unseen objects. Several papers measure the gap precisely — LIBERO-Gen puts the best available model at 21.2% on task compositional generalization — which is progress of a kind. Measuring a gap accurately is the precondition for closing it. But it is not closing it.
